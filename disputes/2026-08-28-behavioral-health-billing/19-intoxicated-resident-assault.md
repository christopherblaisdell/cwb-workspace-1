# 19 — Two Sexual Assaults on Patients; Intoxicated Resident; Victim's Stay Extended

**What you witnessed:** your roommate "Charlie" was so intoxicated he could barely stand,
inside a residential treatment facility. While in that state, he touched your other
roommate inappropriately **while that roommate was asleep**.

---

## ⚠️ Read this first — the person harmed was not you

This is the most sensitive item in the file, and it needs to be handled differently from
the rest.

**Two separate things happened**, and they belong in different places:

| What | Whose it is | Where it goes |
|---|---|---|
| **A resident was sexually touched while asleep** | **The other roommate's.** Not yours. | Law enforcement; the victim's own decision |
| **The facility allowed an intoxicated resident to remain unsupervised with sleeping patients** | **Yours to report** — you were a resident in that room | Regulators, accreditor, your own claim |

**Your standing is clearest on the facility's failure.** That's the part you can and
should report without reservation. The assault itself is reportable too, but the victim's
autonomy has to be respected in how you handle it.

### The hard question: does he know?

He was asleep. **He may not know it happened.**

I'm not going to tell you what to do with that. It's genuinely difficult, and reasonable
people land in different places. But two things are worth saying:

1. **You don't have to be the one to tell him.** If you report to law enforcement, they
   handle notification through proper channels, with training and support you don't have.
   That is usually the better path for everyone.
2. **Don't bury it either.** He was harmed while helpless in a facility that was
   supposed to keep him safe.

**Don't contact him directly.** Not to warn him, not to check on him, not to ask him to
corroborate. It could be characterized as interference or witness contact, it puts you in
an impossible position, and it isn't your role. Report it and let the people whose job it
is take it from there.

## ⭐ The devastating irony: they had cameras in that room

Sit with this for a moment.

**They put a camera in every bedroom.** They displayed the live feeds on monitors in
public view. They surveilled patients in the one place patients had a right to privacy.

**And a resident was assaulted in a bedroom while asleep, and nobody intervened.**

So one of two things is true:

| Scenario | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Staff were watching the monitors** | They saw an obviously intoxicated resident in a room with sleeping patients and did nothing. |
| **Staff were not watching the monitors** | The cameras served no protective purpose at all — they were surveillance without safety, which destroys the only defense they have for having them. |

There is no third option, and **both are catastrophic for them.**

### ⭐ And we now know exactly where that monitor was

The screen displaying the live bedroom feeds is **behind the counter of the nurses'
station** — the staffed post, and the window where every patient collects medication. It
sits at roughly a **90° angle to the counter, oriented toward the staff work area**, while
remaining visible from where patients stand. See
[15-surveillance-cameras.md](15-surveillance-cameras.md).

**The orientation is the point.** The display was positioned for staff to view, at the
position staff occupy. That forecloses any argument that the feed was somewhere staff
could not reasonably have seen it.

**The live video from the bedroom where the assault occurred was displaying on a monitor
angled toward the staff work area at the nurses' station.** And an intoxicated resident
who could barely stand had to reach that bedroom — past that same staffed station.

This is the argument that ties your two strongest facts together:

> "The facility placed cameras in every patient bedroom and displayed the live feeds on a
> monitor at the nurses' station, oriented toward staff and visible to every patient
> collecting medication. It did not use that surveillance to prevent an intoxicated
> resident from assaulting a sleeping patient. The cameras invaded the privacy of every
> resident while delivering none of the protection that is their only justification."

That sentence should appear in your Joint Commission, AHCA, and DCF complaints.

### ⏰ And it may be on video

**The assault may have been recorded.** So may Charlie's obvious intoxication.

Retention is typically 30–90 days. The preservation letter in
[15-surveillance-cameras.md](15-surveillance-cameras.md) now expressly covers your room
for the relevant dates. **Send it now** — this is the single most urgent reason to.

## The facility failures — this is your ground

Independent of the assault, every one of these is a licensure violation:

**1. Contraband entered a residential treatment facility.**
A patient became severely intoxicated inside a locked residential program. Either
substances were brought in and not detected, someone supplied them, or he left and
returned unsearched. All are failures of a core function. **This is a detox facility.**

**2. Obvious intoxication went unnoticed or unaddressed.**
"Could barely stand" is not subtle. Staff either didn't observe it — in a facility with
cameras in every room — or observed it and did nothing.

**3. No medical response.**
Severe intoxication in a detox setting is a **medical emergency**, particularly with
unknown substances and possible interaction with prescribed medications like the Ativan
being administered on that unit. No assessment, no vitals, no monitoring, no transfer.

**4. Failure to protect.**
Facilities have a duty to protect residents from foreseeable harm, including harm from
other residents. An intoxicated resident housed with sleeping patients is foreseeable
risk.

**5. Room assignment and supervision.**
No separation, no one-to-one, no room change, no check.

**6. Probable failure to report.**
See the mandatory reporting section below.

## Where to report

### 1. ⭐⭐⭐ Martin County Sheriff's Office

Sexual contact with a person who is **asleep** is a serious crime in Florida. A sleeping
person cannot consent, and Florida law treats a physically helpless victim as a
significant aggravating circumstance. *Verify the specific offense with law enforcement or
counsel — the classification depends on the nature of the contact, which only you can
describe accurately.*

- You are a **witness**. You may report what you saw.
- They will handle victim notification appropriately.
- **Be precise.** Describe exactly what you observed. Do not characterize it legally —
  don't say "sexual battery" or "assault," say what you saw. Let them classify it.

### 2. ⭐⭐⭐ Florida Abuse Hotline — **1-800-96-ABUSE (1-800-962-2873)**

Florida requires reporting of abuse, neglect, or exploitation of a **vulnerable adult**
(Fla. Stat. ch. 415). A person incapacitated by intoxication or in withdrawal treatment
may qualify as a vulnerable adult. *Verify.*

**Critically: facility staff are mandatory reporters.** Ask whether a report was made.

- **If they reported it** → there's a state record corroborating the event.
- **If they didn't** → failure to report abuse of a vulnerable adult is itself a
  violation, and it becomes the **fourth** instance of this facility documenting or
  observing a serious problem and doing nothing.

You can report anonymously if you prefer. Reports are accepted 24/7.

### 3. ⭐⭐⭐ The Joint Commission — reviewable sentinel event

**Sexual assault of a patient within a healthcare facility is expressly a reviewable
sentinel event** under Joint Commission criteria.

Combined with the stairway fall, you are now reporting **two sentinel events** at the same
accredited facility. That is not a complaint they can triage away.

`jointcommission.org` → Report a Patient Safety Event

### 4. AHCA and DCF

Include in your complaints:
- Contraband present in the facility
- A patient severely intoxicated with no medical response
- Assault on a sleeping patient
- Whether an **adverse incident report** was filed
- Whether the **Abuse Hotline** was called
- That bedroom cameras existed but did not prevent it

**Request an unannounced inspection.** You now have four reasons for one.

## ❓ What I need to know

Only report what you're certain of. Precision protects the case and protects you.

**1. What exactly did you see?**
Describe it plainly and factually — what part of the body, over or under clothing, how
long, what happened next. I know that's uncomfortable to write. **Law enforcement will
need this level of specificity**, and vagueness is what causes reports to go nowhere.

**2. Timing** — date, approximate time, how long it lasted.

**3. Did the sleeping roommate wake up?** Did he react? Did you speak to him afterward?
Does he know?

**4. What did you do?** Did you intervene, say something, leave, report it? *(No wrong
answer. You were a patient in crisis in a room with an intoxicated man. Nobody is
evaluating your response.)*

**5. Did you report it to staff?** To whom, when, what did they say, what changed?
Anything in writing?

**6. Charlie**
- First name only in any complaint — don't publish his full name.
- What was he intoxicated on, if you know?
- How did he get it, if you know?
- Was he a detox patient?
- Did staff see him in that condition?
- Was he moved, discharged, or disciplined?
- Had this happened before?

**7. Was there a camera in your room?** Where was it pointed? Would it have covered the
beds?

**8. Other witnesses** — first names only. **Do not contact them.**

**9. How did it affect you?** Did you feel unsafe sleeping there afterward? Did you stop
sleeping? This one is about **you**, and it may be real damages.

## ⭐⭐⭐ A SECOND incident — and this one may be worse

**Reported (not witnessed):** a male patient put his fingers in or on the genitals of a
female patient **in the common area**. You were told the facility then **required that
female patient to stay in treatment substantially longer because of the incident.**

### ⚠️ First: you did not see this. Label it that way, always.

You heard about it. That is **hearsay**, and you must say so every single time:

> "I did not witness this. It was reported to me by [other residents / a staff member /
> the patient herself] on or about [DATE]."

**Report it anyway.** Regulators are not bound by rules of evidence — DCF, AHCA, and the
Joint Commission investigate reports and can interview staff and residents directly.
Secondhand information from a former resident is a routine and proper basis for a
regulatory referral.

But **never present it as something you saw.** One overstated claim in an eight-issue
complaint contaminates the seven that are ironclad.

### 1. Two sexual assaults destroys "isolated incident"

Your case now describes **two separate sexual assaults on patients** during a single
admission:

| | Where | Victim's state |
|---|---|---|
| Your roommate, by an intoxicated resident | Bedroom | Asleep |
| A female patient | **Common area** | Reported as non-consensual |

One incident is an incident. **Two is a pattern**, and it forecloses the single most
effective defense a facility has: *"an isolated event that could not have been
anticipated."*

And note the second location. A **common area** is the space most likely to be staffed and
observed. If an assault occurred there, supervision failed in the part of the building
where supervision is easiest.

### 2. ⭐ Extending the victim's stay is the alarming part

If the facility kept the female patient longer **because she was assaulted**, that raises
three serious and distinct questions. A regulator has to answer all three.

| Possibility | What it would mean |
|---|---|
| **Clinical** — her stay was extended because the assault caused genuine treatment need | Potentially legitimate — **but only if** individually assessed, clinically documented, and **agreed to by her**. Check whether documentation exists. |
| **Retaliatory or controlling** — she was kept because she was a problem, a witness, or a liability | A patient rights violation, and a serious one. Consequences imposed on a victim for having been victimized. |
| **⭐ Financial** — a longer stay is more billable days | This is where it connects to everything else in your case. |

**Take the third one seriously.** This is a facility that placed a sedated detox patient
upstairs, in violation of its own safety policy, **because it was over census.** You have
already documented that bed occupancy drove a decision that put a patient in a trauma
center.

A facility that extends the stay of an assault victim — a person in no position to
object, with her devices confiscated and no ability to call family — while billing her
insurer for the additional days, has a conflict of interest that regulators are obligated
to examine.

**Ask the question neutrally, and let them investigate:**

> "I was informed that a female patient's length of stay was extended following an
> assault. I ask the agency to determine whether that extension was clinically justified
> and documented, whether she consented to it, whether she was free to leave, and whether
> additional days were billed to her insurer."

That is a fair, precise question. It doesn't accuse anyone of fraud. It simply asks the
one body that can look at the chart and the claims to look.

### 3. Her freedom to leave

If she was a **voluntary** patient who wished to leave and was kept, that is a serious
matter. Add the surrounding facts from this file: **devices confiscated, no contact with
family, no ability to call anyone.**

A voluntary patient with no phone, no outside contact, and no means to arrange
transportation has a meaningfully impaired ability to exercise the right to leave. Whether
that rises to a legal claim is a question for counsel — but the combination is exactly
what regulators look for.

*If a Marchman Act petition was used to hold her, that's a court process with
requirements, and there would be a record of it.*

### 4. ⏰ The common area was almost certainly on camera

They put cameras in **bedrooms.** A common area is far more likely to be covered.

**The assault may be recorded.** So may the aftermath.

The preservation letter in [15-surveillance-cameras.md](15-surveillance-cameras.md)
already demands all footage from anywhere in the facility during your dates of service,
which covers common areas. **Send it now.** If you can approximate the date, tell counsel.

### 5. Reporting

- **Florida Abuse Hotline — 1-800-96-ABUSE.** Report what you were told, clearly labeled
  as secondhand. Ask whether the facility filed a report. Staff are mandatory reporters.
- **Martin County Sheriff's Office.** You can report secondhand information. They decide
  what to do with it. Do not characterize the offense legally — describe what you were
  told, and by whom.
- **Joint Commission.** Sexual assault of a patient is a reviewable sentinel event. You are
  now reporting **three** sentinel-level events at one accredited facility: the stairway
  fall, the bedroom assault, and this.
- **AHCA / DCF.** Add the length-of-stay question above.

### 6. ❓ What I need

- **How did you learn of it?** From other residents, staff, the woman herself, or general
  talk on the unit? *(Be precise — "several residents discussed it openly" is different
  from "a staff member told me.")*
- **When**, approximately?
- **Where in the common area**, and was staff present?
- Did the male patient remain in the facility afterward? Was he moved, discharged, or
  separated?
- Was law enforcement called that you know of?
- **How did you learn her stay was extended?** From her, from staff, from other residents?
- **How much longer?** Days, weeks?
- Did she want to leave?
- Did anyone say *why* she was kept?
- Were residents told anything officially about the incident?
- Did the facility change anything afterward — supervision, common area rules, room
  assignments?

### ⛔ Cautions — these matter more here than anywhere else

- **Do not name her.** Not in a complaint, not in a letter, not to anyone. "A female
  patient."
- **Do not contact her.** Under any circumstances. She is a survivor of a sexual assault
  and she is not a resource for your case.
- **Do not name the male patient in full.** First name only if needed.
- **Do not speculate** about what happened or why she was kept. Report what you were told
  and who told you. Nothing more.
- **Do not post about it anywhere.**

The reason to report this is that a woman may have been assaulted and then held longer for
it, and the agencies with power to look have no way to know unless someone tells them. It
does not add a dollar to your claim, and that is fine.
