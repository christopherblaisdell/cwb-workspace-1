## First: what your answers changed

### ❌ The Establishment Clause is off the table — and that's fine

You went voluntarily, in crisis, to a private facility on private insurance. No court,
probation officer, or state agency compelled you into 12-step programming.

That means the *Kerr / Warner / Inouye / Hazle* line of cases does not reach this
facility. **Do not cite them.** Citing constitutional claims that require state action
against a private provider is the fastest way to get a complaint dismissed as
unsophisticated, and it will cost you credibility on the claims that *are* good.

**One clarifying question:** did any part of this begin with a **Baker Act** involuntary
examination (Fla. Stat. § 394.463) or a **Marchman Act** petition (Ch. 397, Part V)? You
said no one required you to go, but people in crisis are sometimes brought in under one
of those and then convert to voluntary status. If law enforcement, a court, or a
physician's certificate was involved at any point, tell me — that reintroduces a state
actor and changes the analysis.

### ⚠️ Florida's public accommodation law is probably NOT available

I flagged state civil rights law as a strong route generically. In Florida specifically,
it is weaker, and you should know that before you spend effort on it.

The Florida Civil Rights Act (Fla. Stat. ch. 760) prohibits religious discrimination in
places of public accommodation at § 760.08. But Florida's definition of "public
accommodation" in § 760.02 is modeled closely on federal Title II — inns, restaurants,
theaters, sports venues — and **does not clearly cover hospitals or treatment
facilities.** Florida courts have generally read it narrowly.

- **Action:** worth one consult with a Florida civil rights attorney to confirm, but do
  not build the case on it.
- If pursued, the deadline to file with the **Florida Commission on Human Relations** is
  **365 days** from the incident. Don't let that lapse while deciding.

### ✅ What you have instead is better suited to the actual goal

You want the $4,000 gone. Florida gives you three strong routes, and none of them
require a discrimination theory.

## ★ Route 1 — Florida statutory patient rights (the strongest regulatory hook)

Florida codifies patient rights directly in statute. Which chapter applies depends on
the facility's license:

**If licensed as a substance abuse service provider — Fla. Stat. ch. 397:**

- **§ 397.501, "Rights of individuals"** — this is a statutory bill of rights for people
  receiving substance abuse services. It includes a **right to individual dignity**, a
  **right to nondiscriminatory service delivery**, and a **right to quality services**.
  *Verify the exact subsections and whether religion is among the enumerated
  non-discrimination categories.*
- **Fla. Admin. Code r. 65D-30** — the licensure standards for substance abuse services,
  including requirements for **individualized treatment planning** and qualified staff.
  A single-track program that ignores a patient's explicit request is hard to square
  with an individualized-planning requirement.

**If licensed as a mental health facility — Fla. Stat. ch. 394:**

- **§ 394.459, "Rights of patients"** — includes the **right to individual dignity** and
  the **right to treatment**, with express language that patients be treated in a manner
  that respects personal dignity.

> **The "right to individual dignity" provision is the one your incident lands on
> squarely.** A staff member polling residents on their religious beliefs, then turning
> his back on the one who answered "atheist" while physically embracing the others and
> announcing "SOME of us will be praying," is a dignity violation on any reading. It is
> not a close call, and it does not require anyone to accept a contested view of what
> good treatment looks like.

## ★ Route 2 — FDUTPA (Florida Deceptive and Unfair Trade Practices Act)

**Fla. Stat. § 501.201 et seq.** This is genuinely useful and often overlooked.

- Prohibits unfair or deceptive acts in trade or commerce.
- **Allows a private cause of action** — you can sue, not just complain.
- **Prevailing party may recover attorney's fees.** That fee provision is why a Florida
  consumer attorney might take this on contingency for an amount as small as $4,000.
- **Statute of limitations: 4 years.**

The theory: the facility marketed and sold residential treatment. If it represented the
program as evidence-based, clinical, individualized, or CBT-inclusive — on its website,
in its brochure, or through admissions staff — and delivered a single-track religious
program, that is a deceptive practice. Selling clinical treatment and delivering peer
mutual-help meetings at clinical prices is the core of it.

**This is why capturing the website matters so much.** Do it today:
- Save PDFs of every program/treatment/approach page.
- Pull the archived version from `web.archive.org` for your admission date.
- Save any admissions emails or texts.

## Florida records law

- **Fla. Stat. § 456.057** — patient records held by licensed practitioners; must be
  furnished on written request. *Verify the current timeliness language.*
- **Fla. Stat. § 395.3025** — records held by licensed facilities.
- **42 C.F.R. Part 2** — federal confidentiality rules for substance use disorder
  programs. Facilities sometimes invoke Part 2 to *slow* records requests. It restricts
  disclosure **to third parties**; it does not restrict **your access to your own
  records**. If they cite Part 2 to delay you, say so plainly in writing.
- Your **HIPAA 30-day clock** under 45 C.F.R. § 164.524 runs regardless. Keep leading
  with that — it's the cleanest deadline you have.

> **On medical malpractice:** Florida's Ch. 766 presuit process is expensive and
> demanding, and a $4,000 billing dispute is not a malpractice case. I mention it only
> so you know the clock exists if anything more serious happened during that admission.

## One practical Florida warning

**Florida is a two-party consent state** for recording conversations (Fla. Stat.
§ 934.03). Do **not** record a call with the facility or a collector without their
consent — it's a felony and it would wreck your case. Everything in writing is the
better path anyway, which is what this whole file is built around.

## ⭐ Why your incident is the best fact in the case

Read this part carefully, because I don't think you've realized what you have.

> "The instructor asked each of our religious beliefs. When I said I was an atheist, he
> began a prayer with the other people in the group, put his arms around them and his
> back to me, and said *'SOME of us will be praying.'*"

That does three things at once:

**1. It proves the session was not clinical group psychotherapy.**

This is the critical one. A licensed clinician conducting billable group psychotherapy
(`90853`) does not poll patients on their religious beliefs, does not lead group prayer,
and does not physically and verbally exclude a patient from the circle. **Whatever that
session was, it was not psychotherapy.** If the facility billed a clinical code for it,
your own account — corroborated by the roster and the facilitator's credentials — shows
the code was wrong.

You didn't just get treated badly in a group. You witnessed direct evidence that the
group wasn't the clinical service they charged for.

**2. It's a dignity violation with no ambiguity.**

"SOME of us will be praying," said with his back turned, is a staff member publicly
marking a patient as an outsider on religious grounds, in a treatment setting, while
that patient was in crisis. Under Florida's right-to-individual-dignity provisions, a
regulator does not need to weigh competing clinical philosophies to see the problem.

**3. It has witnesses, and they're identifiable.**

Every other resident in that room saw it. The **group attendance roster** for that date
names them. That roster is already covered by your records request in letter #01.

### What to do about it right now

- [ ] **Write it down today** using [10-incident-statement.md](10-incident-statement.md),
      before more time passes. Contemporaneous written accounts carry real weight; a
      recollection produced months later after you've talked to a lawyer carries less.
- [ ] **Pin down the date.** Cross-reference against texts or calls to your wife, the
      program schedule, or a journal. Even "the second Tuesday" narrows it — the roster
      will confirm it.
- [ ] **Write down the facilitator's name and title**, exactly as you knew it. We will
      check whether he held any clinical license.
- [ ] **Do not contact other residents.** It could be construed as witness tampering and
      it raises confidentiality problems. Regulators can interview them; you shouldn't.
- [ ] **Check the group's CPT code** on the itemized bill for that date when it arrives.
