# 05 — Insurance: Cost-Sharing Verification, Parity Challenge, and Appeal

> **Confirmed:** the $4,000 is a **copay** — i.e. cost-sharing the plan assigned to
> you, not a denied claim. That reframes this track. You are not primarily arguing
> "the insurer should have paid." You are arguing **"$4,000 is not the correct
> cost-sharing amount under this plan,"** and separately that a copay this large on a
> behavioral health admission may violate federal parity law.
>
> **Send by:** Certified Mail w/ Return Receipt to the appeals/grievance address on
> your EOB, plus member portal upload.
>
> **⏰ DEADLINE:** Appeals/grievances are typically due **180 days from the EOB date**.
> This clock runs regardless of the facility's stonewalling. File to preserve your

## Why a copay is still very much disputable

People assume cost-sharing is fixed and non-negotiable. It often isn't. Work through
these in order — several of them can zero out or sharply reduce $4,000.

### 1. ⭐ Out-of-pocket maximum — check this first

Every ACA-compliant plan has an annual out-of-pocket maximum. **Once you hit it, your
cost-sharing for covered in-network services drops to $0 for the rest of the plan
year.** A $4,000 residential behavioral health copay very often pushes a member to or
past that limit — and facilities routinely bill the copay anyway, because they bill
from their own contract sheet without checking your accumulator.

- [ ] What is the plan's individual OOP max? $__________
- [ ] What is the family OOP max? $__________
- [ ] How much had already been applied at admission? $__________
- [ ] Does $4,000 exceed what remained? → **If yes, you may owe nothing.**
- [ ] Do the EOBs show cost-sharing accumulating correctly across *all* claims for the
      episode (facility, physician group, and lab may bill separately)?

This is the single highest-value check in this entire file. Do it before anything else.

### 2. ⭐ Does the copay structure on the EOB match the plan document?

A "copay" is normally a **flat fee** — per admission, or per day capped at N days. It
is not usually a percentage. So $4,000 has to come from somewhere specific:

- [ ] Plan document says the residential/inpatient behavioral health copay is:
      $________ per [admission / day], capped at ________ days.
- [ ] Multiply it out. Does it actually equal $4,000? __________
- [ ] If the plan says "$X per admission" but you were charged per *day*, that is a
      billing error.
- [ ] If you were discharged and readmitted, were you charged **two** admission
      copays? Many plans treat readmission within 30–90 days as one admission.
- [ ] Is the facility charging a *coinsurance* percentage while calling it a copay?
      Those are different benefits with different rules.

### 3. ⭐ Mental Health Parity (MHPAEA) — strong argument for a flat copay

Federal parity law prohibits a plan from imposing **financial requirements** on mental
health/substance use benefits that are more restrictive than the *predominant*
requirement applied to **substantially all** medical/surgical benefits in the same
classification (here: inpatient, in-network).

In plain terms: **if your plan's copay for a regular inpatient medical/surgical
admission is $500, it generally cannot charge $4,000 for an inpatient behavioral
health admission.** That's a textbook quantitative treatment limitation violation.

- [ ] What is the plan's copay for an inpatient **medical/surgical** admission?
      $__________
- [ ] What is the plan's copay for inpatient **behavioral health**? $__________
- [ ] Are they different? → **Parity claim. This is the strongest insurance argument
      available to you, and it is badly under-used by consumers.**

Demand the plan's comparative analysis. Since the Consolidated Appropriations Act,
2021, plans must maintain one and produce it on request.

### 4. Was the level of care correctly classified?

Copays differ by level of care. If the facility billed **residential/inpatient** but
delivered something closer to PHP or IOP, the applicable copay tier is lower — and the
same evidence supports your billing dispute in
[04-formal-billing-dispute.md](04-formal-billing-dispute.md). Your two tracks reinforce
each other here.

### 5. In-network contract limits

If the facility is in-network, its contract with the plan **caps** what it may collect
from you at the plan-determined cost-sharing amount. If the facility is billing $4,000
but the EOB says patient responsibility is a different number, the facility is
violating its provider agreement.

- [ ] EOB "patient responsibility" figure: $__________
- [ ] Facility is billing: $4,000.00
- [ ] Do they match? → **If not, report to the insurer's Provider Relations / Network
      Management department. The insurer will lean on the facility, and this is often
      the fastest resolution available.**

### 6. Was it applied to the right plan year?

If the admission spanned a plan-year boundary, deductible and OOP max reset mid-stay.
Verify each date of service was applied to the correct year's accumulator.

### 7. No Surprises Act

If any part of the care was out-of-network, or an out-of-network provider treated you
at an in-network facility, cost-sharing protections and balance-billing prohibitions
may apply.

## Plan type matters

- **Employer self-funded (ERISA):** appeal path is in your Summary Plan Description.
  You have a federal right to *all documents relied upon* free of charge, 29 C.F.R.
  § 2560.503-1. External review still available.
- **Fully insured / marketplace:** state insurance department regulates. External review
  is state-run and binding.
- **Medicaid/Medicare:** different, faster appeal tracks with hard deadlines. Ask for a
  fair hearing.

## After the internal appeal

1. **Second-level internal appeal** if the plan offers one — file it.
2. **External review.** Once internal appeals are exhausted (or the plan misses its own
   deadline), request independent external review. It is **free**, decided by
   physicians with no relationship to the plan, and **binding on the plan**. Request it
   through your state Department of Insurance, or via `externalappeal.cms.gov` for
   federally-administered review.
3. **Expedited review** is available if delay would jeopardize your health.
4. **Also file** with your state Department of Insurance consumer complaint division —
   and, if this is an employer plan, with the **U.S. Department of Labor, Employee
   Benefits Security Administration** (1-866-444-3272). EBSA will contact the plan on
   your behalf and this is often effective.
