# 21 — Should the Monthly Payments Stop?

**Situation:** your wife has been making monthly payments toward the $4,000 while the
underlying debt is disputed and may rest on a forged financial responsibility agreement.

> **This is a real decision with downside in both directions, and it is a legal decision.
> Make it one of the first three questions you ask an attorney.** What follows is the
> analysis so you can decide quickly if you can't reach counsel this week — not a
> directive.

---

## ⚠️ First: one question that changes everything

**Who set up the payment plan, and who signed it?**

| Scenario | Consequence |
|---|---|
| **You "signed"** the payment agreement | Then it's likely another forged document — and it's *evidence*, not an obligation. Get a copy immediately. |
| **Your wife signed** as guarantor or responsible party | ⚠️ **She may have created her own independent obligation**, separate from yours and unaffected by any forgery in your intake paperwork. This materially changes the analysis. |
| **Nobody signed** — she just started paying the statements | Weakest position for them. No contract, just voluntary payments. |

**Find out before doing anything else.** If she signed a guarantor agreement, stopping
payments exposes *her*, not you, and that's a different conversation entirely.

Ask her: *did you sign anything — a payment plan, a financial agreement, an autopay
authorization, anything at the facility or afterward?* And get a copy.

---

## The case for stopping

**1. The debt may not be owed at all.**
If the financial responsibility agreement bears a forged signature, there is no contract.
Every payment made is money paid on an obligation that may not exist.

**2. Money paid is much harder to recover than money withheld.**
To get back what's been paid, you must affirmatively sue and win. To keep what hasn't been
paid, *they* have to come after *you* — and they'd have to produce the signed agreement to
do it. **That asymmetry is the single strongest argument for pausing.**

**3. It's internally inconsistent with your dispute.**
Letter [#01](01-itemized-bill-and-records-request.md) states you dispute the balance in
its entirety. Paying it monthly while disputing it hands them an obvious argument: *"They
disputed it in writing and kept paying anyway."* You want your conduct to match your
position.

**4. Leverage.**
Payments coming in reliably every month remove any urgency for them to respond to your
letters. They already ignore your phone calls. Money arriving anyway confirms that
ignoring you works.

**5. It may be arguable as acknowledgment.**
Continued payment can be characterized as accepting the charges as valid. *(Note: under
Fla. Stat. § 95.04 an acknowledgment extending a time-barred debt generally must be in
writing — and this debt isn't time-barred anyway. So the statute-of-limitations concern is
weaker than internet advice suggests. The real risk is the appearance, not the statute.)*

## The case for caution

**1. Stopping may trigger a collections referral.**
Realistically likely at some point regardless, given you've disputed it. And it's not pure
downside — a collector triggers your **FDCPA validation rights**
([07](07-collections-debt-validation.md)), forcing a third party to demand substantiation
the facility has never provided.

**2. Credit reporting.**
$4,000 is above the $500 medical-debt reporting floor. But there's a **one-year delay**
before medical collections can appear, and properly disputed debt must be reported as
disputed. Your letters create that record.

**3. If your wife is contractually bound**, stopping is a breach by *her*. See above.

**4. A payment plan may contain an acceleration clause** — missing a payment could make
the full balance immediately due. Read the agreement if one exists.

**5. Small risk of suit.** For $4,000 they'd more likely sell the debt than sue. And if
they did sue, they would have to produce the signed agreement in court — which, given the
forgery issue, is arguably somewhere you'd *want* them to go.

---

## ⭐ The better option: don't just stop — suspend, in writing

Silently stopping looks like delinquency. **Formally suspending payments pending the
dispute looks like a documented position**, and it puts the reason on the record.

That distinction matters if this ever reaches a collector, a credit bureau, a regulator,
or a judge.

## Practical steps if you suspend

- [ ] **Find out who signed what** before anything else.
- [ ] **Total up what's been paid.** Exact figure, with dates and methods. This is both a
      damages item and something you may seek returned.
- [ ] **Cancel autopay at the source.** If it's a recurring card or bank debit, cancel it
      with your bank or card issuer as well as notifying the facility — don't rely on them
      to stop it. *(Ask your bank about a stop payment or revoking the authorization.)*
- [ ] **Don't cancel a card in a way that hides other charges** you may need to trace.
- [ ] **Keep every receipt and statement** showing payments made.
- [ ] **Send the notice above** — don't stop silently.
- [ ] **Watch the mail** for collection letters. If one arrives, you have **30 days** to
      demand validation ([07](07-collections-debt-validation.md)).
- [ ] **Check your credit report** in a few months. `annualcreditreport.com`, free.

## ⛔ And do not

- **Don't let your wife sign anything new** — a revised payment plan, a settlement, a
  hardship application that includes an acknowledgment of the debt, or an autopay
  authorization. Any of those could ratify an obligation that may not exist.
- **Don't make a "good faith" partial payment** to smooth things over.
- **Don't negotiate over the phone.**

---

## My honest read

**If your wife has not personally signed anything assuming liability**, the case for
suspending is strong — primarily because of the asymmetry: withheld money stays yours by
default, paid money has to be won back.

**If she has signed something**, get a lawyer's view before stopping. Her exposure is a
different question from yours and the forgery may not help her.

Either way, **do it in writing**. And put it near the top of your first attorney
conversation — every month that passes is more money out the door on a debt that may never
have been validly incurred.
