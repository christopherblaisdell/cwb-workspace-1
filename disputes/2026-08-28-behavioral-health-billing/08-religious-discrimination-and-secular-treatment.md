# 08 — Religious Programming, Denial of Secular Treatment, and Exclusion from Group

**Core facts (to be confirmed and particularized):**

1. The facility's primary programming was 12-step based, which is religious in content
   ("God," "Higher Power," prayer, surrender of will to a deity).
2. No secular, evidence-based alternative (CBT, SMART Recovery, MI, relapse prevention)
   was offered.
3. The patient was **excluded from at least one group treatment session on the basis of
   religion** — i.e., because he is an atheist.

This is materially stronger than a generic "the care was inadequate" complaint, for one
central reason: **it is objective and verifiable.** It does not require a regulator to
agree with a subjective judgment about treatment quality. Either the facility offered a
secular track or it didn't. Either the patient was excluded from a group or he wasn't.
Either the schedule shows 12-step meetings where clinical psychotherapy was billed, or
it doesn't.

---

## ⚖️ The legal grounds, ranked honestly

Some of these are strong; some are frequently overstated online. Do not lead with the
weak ones — it damages credibility with regulators.

### 1. ★★★ Billed for clinical psychotherapy, delivered 12-step meetings

**This is the ground that gets the money back.** It converts a values dispute into a
billing-accuracy dispute, which is where you have the most leverage.

Residential SUD/behavioral health per-diems and group therapy codes are billed on the
premise that a **licensed clinician** is delivering **psychotherapy**. Specifically:

| Code | What it requires |
|---|---|
| `90853` | Group **psychotherapy** by a qualified licensed clinician |
| `90832 / 90834 / 90837` | Individual psychotherapy, 30 / 45 / 60 min, licensed clinician |
| `H0035`, `H2036`, `H0018/H0019` | Program-level codes presuming clinical programming |
| Residential per-diem (rev. codes `1001`, `0116`, `0126`, etc.) | Presumes a clinically staffed program |

**A 12-step meeting is not billable psychotherapy.** AA/NA-format meetings are peer-led
mutual-help groups. They are free in every community in America. They are not a
clinical service, they are not delivered by a licensed clinician acting clinically, and
they cannot lawfully be billed to insurance as group psychotherapy.

So the question for the audit in
[04-formal-billing-dispute.md](04-formal-billing-dispute.md) becomes very concrete:

- For each group billed as `90853`, **who facilitated it**, and what license did they
  hold? A peer/recovery coach/tech is not a psychotherapy provider.
- Was the content a 12-step meeting (reading the Big Book, step work, prayer) or actual
  psychotherapy?
- **Was the patient billed for the group he was excluded from?**
- Does the daily schedule in the record show 12-step meetings occupying the hours the
  per-diem was billed for?

If clinical codes were billed for 12-step meetings, that is billing for services not
rendered. If any of it touched Medicaid or Medicare, it is a False Claims Act issue.

### 2. ★★★ State facility licensing — patient rights and individualized treatment

Nearly every state's behavioral health licensing regulations contain a **patient bill of
rights** that includes freedom of religion and freedom from religious coercion, plus a
requirement of an **individualized treatment plan**. A program that offers exactly one
ideological track, and excludes a patient from treatment for not sharing it, violates
both.

This is actionable at the licensing agency **regardless** of whether the admission was
voluntary or court-ordered, and regardless of the Establishment Clause analysis below.
It is the most reliably available regulatory hook.

### 3. ★★★ Accreditation standards (Joint Commission / CARF)

Both accreditors require:
- Care that respects the patient's **cultural, spiritual, and personal values**
- **Individualized** treatment planning responsive to patient needs and preferences
- Use of **evidence-based** practice
- An accessible **grievance** process

Exclusion from a treatment group on religious grounds is a patient-rights violation
under these standards. Accreditors take complaints seriously because accreditation is
tied to reimbursement.

### 4. ★★★ State public accommodation / civil rights law

**This is likely your strongest discrimination claim, and it is state-specific.**

Many state civil rights acts prohibit discrimination on the basis of religion or
"creed" in places of public accommodation, and many of them **expressly include
healthcare facilities, clinics, and hospitals**. Non-belief is generally protected on
the same terms as belief.

Notably, some states attach **statutory minimum damages per violation** — California's
Unruh Civil Rights Act, for example, provides a statutory minimum of $4,000 per
violation, which would be an interesting number in this matter.

**I need to know your state to identify the operative statute and agency.**

### 5. ★★ Consumer protection / deceptive practices

If the facility's marketing, website, admission materials, or intake staff represented
that it provided **evidence-based**, **CBT**, or **individualized** treatment, and what
was actually delivered was a single-track 12-step program, that is a misrepresentation
claim for your state Attorney General. Preserve the website — use the Wayback Machine
(`web.archive.org`) to capture how they advertised the program on your admission date,
before they change it.

### 6. ★★ Insurance: medical necessity and what the plan paid for

Your plan paid for **clinical treatment**. If the record shows the programming was
predominantly non-clinical mutual-help meetings, the insurer has been billed for a
level of care it did not receive — and your $4,000 copay is cost-sharing on services
not delivered. The insurer's **Special Investigations Unit** has an independent
financial motive to care about this. Copy them.

### 7. ⚠️ Establishment Clause — powerful, but ONLY with state action

There is a strong, well-settled line of federal case law holding that **compelled**
participation in 12-step programming violates the Establishment Clause, because
AA/NA are religious in nature as a matter of law:

- *Kerr v. Farrey*, 95 F.3d 472 (7th Cir. 1996)
- *Warner v. Orange County Dep't of Probation*, 115 F.3d 1068 (2d Cir. 1997)
- *Griffin v. Coughlin*, 88 N.Y.2d 674 (1996)
- *Inouye v. Kemna*, 504 F.3d 705 (9th Cir. 2007)
- *Hazle v. Crofoot*, 727 F.3d 983 (9th Cir. 2013) — damages awarded to an atheist
  compelled into a religious-based program

**The critical limitation:** these cases require **state action**. They apply where a
court, probation officer, parole board, drug court, child welfare agency, professional
licensing board, or state-run facility *compelled* the religious programming.

**If your admission was fully voluntary at a private facility paid by private
insurance, the Establishment Clause does not directly apply to the facility.** Claiming
otherwise will make a regulator discount everything else you say.

→ **See the questions below. This is the single fact that determines whether this
ground is available.**

### 8. ❌ Grounds that do NOT apply — do not cite these

Being accurate here protects your credibility:

- **ACA Section 1557** prohibits discrimination in federally funded health programs on
  the basis of race, color, national origin, sex, age, and disability. **Religion is not
  a protected category under § 1557.** Do not cite it.
- **Title II of the Civil Rights Act of 1964** (42 U.S.C. § 2000a) covers religion, but
  its definition of "public accommodation" is limited to inns, restaurants, theaters,
  and similar venues. **Healthcare and treatment facilities are generally not covered.**
  Use *state* public accommodation law instead.
- **Title VII** is employment only. Not applicable.
- **ADA** covers disability, not religion. (Though note: SUD in recovery can itself be a
  protected disability — a separate issue.)

Add these to the audit in [04-formal-billing-dispute.md](04-formal-billing-dispute.md):

- [ ] **Daily/weekly program schedule** — how many hours were 12-step vs. clinical?
- [ ] **Group attendance rosters** — do they show you absent from the group you were

- [ ] **Your treatment plan** — does it document your stated preferences and beliefs?
      Does it document any consideration of a secular modality? A plan that ignores a

      both are useful to you.
- [ ] **Incident reports or grievance records** relating to the exclusion.
- [ ] **The facility's own policies** on patient rights, religious accommodation, and
      grievances — request these explicitly.

## The framing to use — important

Do **not** frame this as "I object to religion" or as a philosophical grievance. Frame
it in the facility's own clinical and contractual terms. Three sentences that work:

> "The facility billed my insurer for clinical group psychotherapy. What was delivered
> during those hours was 12-step mutual-help programming, which is not a billable
> clinical service and was not delivered by a licensed clinician. I was additionally
> excluded from a treatment group I was billed for, on the basis of my religious
> beliefs."

That is a billing and patient-rights complaint that happens to involve religion. It is
far more effective with a licensing board, an insurer, and a court than a complaint
that reads as an ideological objection — and it is also simply the more accurate
description of what went wrong.
