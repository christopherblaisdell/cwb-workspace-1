# 24 — False Group Attendance Records

**Reported:** staff asked residents to **sign a paper stating they had attended a group
meeting when no such meeting took place.**

## ⭐⭐⭐ The verbatim account — write this down today, exactly

> Residents would be **swimming in the pool**, or **sitting on the couch watching TV**.
> Staff would bring a paper over and say:
>
> ### *"It looks like you are having your own little group here. Sign this paper."*

**Preserve that sentence word for word.** It is the most damaging single piece of evidence
you have given me, and here is precisely why.

### 1. It shows knowledge — which is what separates fraud from error

A billing *error* is unknowing. **Fraud requires knowledge.**

Nobody says *"it looks like you're having your own little group here"* while believing a
therapy session is underway. The phrasing is a knowing euphemism. It is the language of a
person who understands that what is in front of them is **not** a group, and who is
constructing a rationalisation out loud while handing over the paper.

That sentence is the facility's own staff acknowledging, in real time, that the document
did not reflect reality.

**In a fraud analysis, that is the whole ballgame.** Intent is normally the hardest element
to prove. Here it is embedded in the sentence they used.

### 2. The activities leave no room to argue

This is what makes the quote unanswerable. There is no interpretive space:

| What was happening | Could it conceivably be billed as `90853` group psychotherapy? |
|---|---|
| **Swimming in a pool** | No |
| **Sitting on a couch watching television** | No |

A defence lawyer can argue that a twelve-step meeting had therapeutic value. Nobody can
argue that swimming was group psychotherapy delivered by a licensed clinician. **The facts
foreclose the argument entirely.**

### 3. It was routine, not isolated

"They **would** bring a paper" — habitual, repeated, unremarkable. A staff member with a
practised line, delivered casually enough that it clearly wasn't the first time.

**That indicates institutional practice, not a rogue employee.** Which is exactly what
regulators and a qui tam attorney need to see.

### 4. Every resident present is a witness — and every roster is an exhibit

Multiple residents were in the pool or on the couch each time. **Each of them was asked to
sign the same sheet.**

So for every one of these, there exists a roster bearing **multiple patient signatures** for
a group that did not occur — and multiple people who can say so.

### 5. ⭐ It ties directly to their own marketing

From the facility website, captured in evidence:

> "Relax by our **sparkling pool**, unwind under the **tiki hut**, or participate in fun
> outdoor activities like **basketball** on our 3-on-3 court."

And from testimonials they publish:

> "**Josh the activities director** always had fun activities like **ping pong, tye dye,
> basketball and even ice cream nights**."

The facility advertises recreation as an amenity. **The question for regulators and the
insurer is whether that same recreation was billed as clinical group therapy.**

**Ask it directly:**

> "Were recreational activities — swimming, television, ping pong, tie-dye, basketball —
> documented and billed as group therapy sessions? Residents were approached during
> recreation and asked to sign group attendance sheets."

Note also who is described as running the activities: an **Activities Director**, not a
licensed clinician. If activity time was billed under a psychotherapy code, the facilitator
credential problem in [08](08-religious-discrimination-and-secular-treatment.md) compounds
the false-roster problem here.

## ⭐⭐⭐ Why this is the single most important fact in the case

Everything else in this file establishes that the facility was badly run. **This
establishes that it billed for services it did not provide** — and it does so with the
facility's own documents, signed by the patients themselves.

Group therapy is billed under `90853` and similar codes. **The attendance roster is the
proof of service.** It is the document that says: this group happened, these patients were
there, therefore this claim is payable.

If residents were asked to sign rosters while swimming, then the rosters supporting those
claims are **false records created to substantiate billing for services not rendered.**

That is not a quality-of-care complaint. That is the mechanism of the fraud, described from
the inside, in the staff's own words.

### It answers your original question

You came to this asking whether you should have to pay $4,000. **If the groups you were
billed for did not happen, the charges are void** — and your copay, which is a share of
those charges, goes with them.

You no longer need to argue that twelve-step meetings weren't clinical psychotherapy
([08](08-religious-discrimination-and-secular-treatment.md)), although that argument still
stands. **Some of the billed sessions were people in a swimming pool.**

## The legal exposure

### 1. Billing for services not rendered

Charges supported by falsified attendance records are not payable. This voids the claims,
your copay, and any balance said to be owed.

### 2. Insurance fraud — Fla. Stat. § 817.234

Submitting claims supported by false documentation is criminal in Florida. *Verify.*

**Notify the insurer's Special Investigations Unit.** They have their own investigators, a
direct financial motive, and subpoena power you don't have. Whichever carrier is yours —
BCBS, Cigna, UnitedHealthcare, Aetna, Magellan — has an SIU.

### 3. ⭐⭐ Federal False Claims Act — if any federal money was involved

**This is potentially the largest financial dimension of your entire case, and it needs a
specialist.**

If **Medicare, Medicaid, TRICARE, or any federally funded program** paid any part of any
patient's care during the period false rosters were used, the facility may be liable under
the federal **False Claims Act**, which provides treble damages and per-claim penalties.

The FCA permits a private individual with original knowledge — a **qui tam relator** — to
bring the case on the government's behalf and receive a **share of the recovery**
(generally 15–30%).

**You may qualify.** You were asked directly to sign a false record. That is firsthand
original knowledge.

⚠️ **Critical practical points, and please take these seriously:**

- **The first-to-file rule.** Only the first relator to file on a given set of facts can
  recover. If a former employee files before you, your claim is barred. **This is a reason
  to move promptly.**
- **Filings are made under seal** and served on the DOJ. There is a specific procedure and
  you cannot do it yourself.
- **Public disclosure can bar a claim.** Do **not** post about this publicly, do not
  discuss it in online reviews, and be careful what you put in non-confidential
  correspondence. *(Regulatory complaints are a different question — ask counsel about
  sequencing before you file anything describing the false rosters.)*
- **You need an FCA / qui tam attorney specifically.** This is a specialised bar. General
  consumer lawyers do not handle it.

> ⛔ **Before you file the DCF, AHCA, or Joint Commission complaints, ask a qui tam attorney
> whether describing the false attendance records in those filings could create a public
> disclosure problem.** The other complaints — cameras, the fall, the food, the assaults —
> are almost certainly fine to file now. This one item may need sequencing advice first.
>
> **That is a genuine conflict between two of my recommendations, and I want you to see it
> rather than trip over it.** The cameras and the stairway are urgent because physical
> evidence disappears. The false rosters may benefit from a lawyer's guidance before
> disclosure. You can file the others now and hold this one item for a week while you get
> that advice.

### 4. Falsification of patient records

Independently a licensure violation, reportable to DCF and AHCA, and to the Florida
Department of Health as to any licensed individual who directed it.

### 5. Joint Commission

Records integrity is fundamental to accreditation. This is not a citation-level finding.

## ⛔ Strategy: say nothing until they produce the rosters

**Exactly as with the forged signatures.**

Letter [#01](01-itemized-bill-and-records-request.md) already demands:

> "All individual therapy notes and all group therapy notes, including **group attendance
> rosters and sign-in sheets** showing which groups I attended, the dates, times, duration,
> and the name and credential of the facilitator."

That request is neutral on its face. **Send it as written.**

If they produce rosters bearing your signature for groups that never occurred, you have
them in a fixed position with a document in hand. If you announce the problem first, the
rosters become unavailable, incomplete, or "still being located."

**Get the documents first.**

## ❓ What I need from you

**1. Did you sign them?**
   - [ ] Yes — how many times, roughly?
   - [ ] Sometimes
   - [ ] Refused — what happened?

*(If you signed, that is completely understandable. You were a patient in crisis, dependent
on the facility, being asked by staff. It does not hurt your credibility — it is exactly
how this works, and saying so plainly is stronger than pretending you resisted.)*

**2. Which groups, and when?** Any specific dates or group names you can recall. Even
"twice during my second week" narrows it. **This is the highest-value detail** — a roster
for a date you can say confidently had no group is direct evidence.

**3. How was it presented?**
   - What exactly did staff say? Verbatim if possible.
   - Was it framed as routine paperwork, catching up, a formality?
   - Was it the same staff member each time?
   - **Name and role of whoever asked** — this matters a great deal.

**4. What did the paper look like?** A sign-in sheet with multiple names? A single form? Did
it list a group name, date, time, or facilitator? Were other residents' signatures already
on it?

**5. Did other residents sign?** Did anyone refuse? Did anyone comment on it?

**6. Was there any real group that day**, or nothing at all? Did a group get cancelled and
the sheet circulated anyway?

**7. How often did it happen?** Once, occasionally, routinely?

**8. Did you ever raise it?** With whom, and what was said?

**9. Was this connected to your written complaint** about the refrigerator, or separate?
