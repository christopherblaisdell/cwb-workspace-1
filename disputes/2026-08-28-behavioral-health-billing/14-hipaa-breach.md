# 14 — HIPAA / 42 C.F.R. Part 2 Breach: Records Sent to the Wrong Recipient

**Reported:** the facility disclosed your medical records to the wrong place.

---

## Why this is worse than an ordinary HIPAA breach

Remedy Therapy is a **substance use disorder treatment provider**. Your records are
therefore protected not only by HIPAA but by **42 C.F.R. Part 2**, the federal
confidentiality rule for SUD patient records — which is **stricter than HIPAA**.

Part 2 exists specifically because disclosure of addiction treatment records causes
concrete harm: employment, custody, housing, licensure, insurance, reputation. It
generally prohibits disclosure **without your specific written consent**, and even the
fact that you are a patient is protected.

Since the CAA 2021 alignment, **Part 2 violations carry HIPAA-equivalent civil and
criminal penalties** and are enforced by HHS OCR. *Verify current enforcement posture.*

So this isn't "an address error." It's disclosure of federally protected addiction
treatment records to an unauthorized party.

---

## ⭐ How this connects to the forged signatures

**This is the most important paragraph in this file.**

A disclosure of SUD records normally requires a **signed written consent / release of
information (ROI)** form from you, specifying who may receive the records and what may
be disclosed.

You say you **never signed the intake paperwork** — staff signed your name.

So one of two things is true, and both are bad for them:

| Scenario | Consequence |
|---|---|
| **They disclosed with no authorization at all** | Straightforward Part 2 and HIPAA violation. |
| **They disclosed relying on an authorization bearing a signature you didn't write** | The disclosure was made under a **forged consent form**. That is a Part 2 violation *and* evidence of forgery in the same document. |

Either way, **demand the authorization form they relied upon.** If they produce one with
your name on it, you now have a specific forged instrument that was actually *used* to
disclose federally protected records — which is far more concrete than a forged
financial agreement sitting in a file.

Add this to the document requests. It's now in letter
[#01](01-itemized-bill-and-records-request.md).

## Their obligations — and what they've likely already failed

### Breach Notification Rule (45 C.F.R. §§ 164.400–414)

- They must notify **you** without unreasonable delay and **no later than 60 days** from
  discovery of the breach.
- They must notify **HHS**. If the breach affected 500+ individuals, HHS is notified
  immediately and media notice is required; smaller breaches are logged annually.
- Notice must describe what happened, what information was involved, what they're doing,
  and what you should do.

**Did you ever receive a written breach notification?** If not, that is a **second,
independent violation** on top of the disclosure itself.

### Florida Information Protection Act (Fla. Stat. § 501.171)

Florida requires notice of breaches of personal information — which includes medical and
health insurance information — generally within **30 days**, with Attorney General
notification for larger breaches. Enforced by the Florida AG. *Verify applicability.*

### Accounting of disclosures (45 C.F.R. § 164.528)

You have a **separate federal right** to an accounting of disclosures — a list of
disclosures of your PHI over the prior six years. This is distinct from your right of
access, and most patients never invoke it.

**This is the tool for this problem.** It forces them to document, in writing, every
place your records went. Now added to letter #01.

## ⚠️ Honest limitation: you cannot sue under HIPAA

**There is no private right of action under HIPAA or Part 2.** You cannot file a lawsuit
"for a HIPAA violation." Anyone who tells you otherwise is wrong, and asserting it will
undercut your credibility.

What you can do:

| Route | Notes |
|---|---|
| **HHS OCR complaint** | The real enforcement path. **180-day deadline** from when you knew or should have known. |
| **Florida negligence claim** | HIPAA/Part 2 can establish the **standard of care** in a state negligence action. Many courts allow this. |
| **Breach of confidentiality** | Florida recognizes claims against providers for improper disclosure of medical information. |
| **Invasion of privacy** | Public disclosure of private facts, if the disclosure was to a party who would find it offensive/harmful. |
| **Fla. Stat. § 456.057 / § 397.501** | Florida's own patient records confidentiality provisions. |
| **FDUTPA** | Possible, depending on facts. |
| **DCF / AHCA / Joint Commission** | Licensure and accreditation consequences. |
| **Florida AG** | FIPA breach-notice failures. |

**Damages matter.** A claim is much stronger if the disclosure caused actual harm — lost
job, lost housing, custody consequences, reputational injury, or documented emotional
distress. Write down any consequence you can trace to it.

## The pattern this creates

Step back and look at what a regulator now sees:

1. They **won't give you your own records** despite repeated written requests.
2. They **gave your records to someone else** who wasn't entitled to them.
3. They **signed your name** to the paperwork governing both.

That is not three unrelated errors. It is a **systemic failure of records governance and
patient consent** at a Joint Commission–accredited facility. Framed that way — and it is
the accurate framing — this stops being one patient's billing complaint and becomes a
compliance problem the facility has to take seriously.

Lead with that framing in the DCF, AHCA, and Joint Commission complaints.
