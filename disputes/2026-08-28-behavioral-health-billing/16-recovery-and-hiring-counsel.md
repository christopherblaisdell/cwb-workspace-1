# 16 — What Can Actually Be Recovered, and How to Hire Counsel

> **Honest framing up front.** I am not a lawyer and cannot value your case. What follows
> is a realistic map of where money could come from, what each route is actually worth,
> and what could go wrong. Verify everything with a Florida attorney. Some of these
> theories are strong; some are weaker than they look; one of them depends on a fact you
> haven't confirmed yet.

---

## Short answer

**Yes, probably more than $4,000 — possibly substantially more.** But the range is wide,
and the difference between "the bill goes away" and "a meaningful recovery" turns on
three things:

1. **Whether the cameras captured audio.** This is the single biggest financial variable
   in your case. See below.
2. **Whether the forged signatures can be established.**
3. **Whether this becomes a class case** because of the facility-wide camera practice.

---

## ❌ First, correcting two common misconceptions

**1. OCR fines don't pay you.**
If HHS fines Remedy for the HIPAA and Part 2 violations, that money goes to the federal
government. You get nothing from it. OCR enforcement is valuable to you as *leverage* and
as *findings a lawyer can use* — not as a payout.

**2. You cannot sue for a HIPAA violation.**
No private right of action. Any recovery for the misdirected fax runs through **Florida
state-law claims** that use HIPAA to help establish the standard of care.

## Where money could actually come from

Ranked by realistic value, not by how outrageous the conduct feels.

### 1. ⭐⭐⭐ The audio question — Fla. Stat. § 934.10

**If those bedroom cameras recorded audio, this becomes the dominant claim in your case.**

Florida's interception statute provides a **civil cause of action with liquidated
statutory damages** — meaning you don't have to prove you were harmed a specific amount.
*Verify current figures*, but § 934.10 has historically provided actual damages **or**
liquidated damages computed per day of violation with a statutory floor, **plus punitive
damages, plus attorney's fees and costs.**

Run that across a residential stay, and then across **every patient in every bedroom**,
and the exposure becomes very large very quickly.

**This is why I keep asking about audio.** Check for a microphone port, a small hole near
the lens, any indication in the equipment. If you can't tell, a lawyer can find out in
discovery — but tell them it's a priority question from day one.

### 2. ⭐⭐⭐ Intrusion upon seclusion — the cameras

Florida recognizes this tort. Cameras in a bedroom, without consent, with feeds displayed
publicly, is close to a textbook fact pattern.

- Damages include **emotional distress** — no physical injury required for this tort.
- **Punitive damages** are available where conduct is sufficiently egregious. Florida
  requires court permission to plead them (Fla. Stat. § 768.72) and caps them (§ 768.73,
  generally 3× compensatory or $500,000, with higher limits in some circumstances).
- The **public display** is what elevates this. Private safety monitoring is defensible;
  broadcasting bedrooms to a hallway is not.

**Individually:** meaningful, but depends on how a jury values the intrusion.
**As a class:** this is the theory that makes a firm take the case.

### 3. ⭐⭐ Breach of confidentiality — the misdirected fax

Ordinarily Florida's **impact rule** blocks emotional distress damages without physical
impact. **There is a well-known exception for breach of confidentiality by mental health
providers** — *Gracey v. Eaker*, 837 So. 2d 348 (Fla. 2002), where the Florida Supreme
Court held the impact rule did not bar emotional distress damages against a
psychotherapist who disclosed confidential information. *Verify applicability to your
facts.*

That matters a great deal here, because your damages from the fax are almost entirely
emotional and reputational.

**Strengtheners:** the law office witness, the fax header proving scope, the absence of
any breach notification, and the fact that these are **addiction treatment records**,
which Congress singled out for heightened protection precisely because disclosure causes
this kind of harm.

### 4. ⭐⭐ FDUTPA — Fla. Stat. § 501.201

**The value here is the fee-shifting, not the damages.**

- FDUTPA gives **actual (economic) damages** and, importantly, **attorney's fees to the
  prevailing party**.
- But FDUTPA generally **does not** allow recovery for emotional distress or personal
  injury. So your FDUTPA damages are largely the money itself — the $4,000, and arguably
  the value gap between the individualized, CBT-inclusive program they advertised and the
  single-track program delivered.

**Why it still matters enormously:** the fee provision is what makes a lawyer willing to
take a case this size. It shifts your legal costs onto them if you win.

The evidence is already strong: their website advertises **"cognitive-behavioral
therapy"**, discloses no religious programming, and publishes a testimonial about a
Christian patient getting **his own Bible study group**.

### 5. ⭐⭐ The $4,000 itself — and the forged signature

This is the **most likely** recovery and the easiest.

If the financial responsibility agreement bears a forged signature, **there is no
contract**, and there is no basis to charge you anything. This isn't a discount argument
— it's a complete defense.

Separately, if they billed clinical psychotherapy codes for 12-step meetings run by
unlicensed staff, the charges themselves were improper.

**Realistic expectation: the $4,000 goes away.** Treat that as the floor, not the goal.

### 6. ⭐ Religious discrimination

Real, and it drives regulatory pressure — but as a **direct money claim** it's your
weakest route, because Florida's public accommodation statute likely doesn't cover
treatment facilities (see [09](09-florida-agencies-and-law.md)).

Its value is as **evidence of a pattern** and as leverage. Don't build the financial case
on it.

### 7. ⭐⭐ Class action — the camera practice

Cameras in **every** bedroom with public feeds affected **every resident**, continuously.

- This is what turns a $4,000 dispute into something a firm will invest in.
- **Realistic caveat:** individual class members typically recover modest amounts. Named
  plaintiffs sometimes receive a service award. The lawyers recover fees.
- **It also takes years.** Two to four is common.
- Your individual claims may be worth more pursued individually than as part of a class.
  A lawyer will advise on this — it's a genuine strategic fork.

## ⚠️ What could go wrong — read this part carefully

I'd be doing you a disservice if I only listed upside.

**1. They will scrutinize you.**
You were a patient in addiction treatment, admitted in crisis. Defense counsel will
examine your condition at admission, your medication, your memory, and your history —
not because it's fair, but because it's what they do. Your account of the group incident
and the cameras will be tested against a chart written by the people you're accusing.

This is survivable. It is also genuinely unpleasant, and you should go in knowing it.

**2. The forged signature has to be proven.**
"I don't remember signing" is much weaker than "that is not my handwriting." You'll
likely need a **questioned-documents examiner**. That's why the signature exemplars in
[12](12-forged-signatures.md) matter.

**3. The records are written by them.**
If the chart says you consented, participated, and were offered alternatives, you're
arguing against a document. This is exactly why the **fax header page count**, the **law
office's independent statement**, and any **physical evidence of the cameras** are so
valuable — they don't come from Remedy.

**4. Time.**
A regulatory complaint may produce results in months. Litigation is measured in years.

**5. They may simply write off the $4,000 early.**
If they do, they'll likely want a **full release** in exchange. Do **not** sign one
without counsel — you'd be giving up the camera, privacy, and forgery claims to settle a
bill that may not be owed anyway. This is a real risk and I want you watching for it.

**6. Collectability.**
Check whether they carry insurance and whether the entity has assets. A judgment against
a shell LLC is worth nothing. Sunbiz plus a lawyer's asset check answers this early.

## How to hire the right lawyer

### What kind

You want a **plaintiff's firm** comfortable with **privacy litigation** and ideally
**class actions** — not a general practitioner and not a medical malpractice specialist.
Your case is privacy and consumer, not malpractice.

Good search terms: *Florida privacy class action attorney*, *Florida consumer protection
FDUTPA attorney*, *Florida patient rights attorney*.

### Where to start

| Source | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Disability Rights Florida** — 1-800-342-0823 | **Call first.** Free, and patient rights in behavioral health facilities is literally their mandate. They may take it or refer you well. |
| **Florida Bar Lawyer Referral** — 1-800-342-8011 | Low-cost initial consults |
| Florida privacy / class action firms | Many offer free consults; contingency |
| **FFRF, American Atheists, ACLU-FL** | See [13](13-satanic-temple-outreach.md) |
| **TST Grey Faction** | Mental health industry accountability is their specific focus |

Contact **several**. Firms decline for reasons that have nothing to do with your case's
merit — conflicts, caseload, practice fit.

### Fee structures

- **Contingency** — they take a percentage (commonly 33–40%) and you pay nothing up
  front. Standard for this kind of case.
- **Fee-shifting** — FDUTPA and § 934.10 both allow fees against the defendant. Point
  this out; it makes your case more attractive.
- **Costs vs. fees** — ask specifically who pays *costs* (filing, experts, depositions)
  if you lose. This is where people get surprised.
- **Never pay a large up-front retainer** for a case like this without a very good reason.

### Questions to ask in the consult

1. Have you handled privacy or surveillance claims against healthcare facilities?
2. What do you think of the § 934.10 audio angle if audio was captured?
3. Individual claims or class — which serves me better, and why?
4. What's your read on the forged signature? Do we need a documents examiner?
5. Does FDUTPA fee-shifting change your view of taking this?
6. What happens to my $4,000 balance while this is pending? Collections? Credit?
7. Who pays costs if we lose?
8. Realistically, how long?
9. What's the biggest weakness in my case?

> **Question 9 is the most important one.** A lawyer who only tells you how strong your
> case is, is selling. A good one will tell you exactly where you're exposed.

### What to bring

- This entire case file (print the README plus files 08–16)
- Your signed incident statement ([10](10-incident-statement.md))
- Signature exemplars
- Archived website captures — the CBT claim and the Bible-study testimonial
- All bills, EOBs, insurance documents
- The law office's contact information
- Your damages log

You are walking in far better prepared than almost any client. That matters — it makes
you easier to represent and it makes the case easier to evaluate quickly.

## What I'd actually expect

Setting aside best-case scenarios:

| Outcome | Likelihood |
|---|---|
| **The $4,000 is eliminated or sharply reduced** | Very likely |
| Regulatory findings against the facility | Likely, given four independent issues |
| A negotiated settlement above the bill amount | Plausible — depends on counsel and their insurer's appetite |
| Significant recovery via § 934.10 | **Depends entirely on whether audio was captured** |
| Class action, if the camera practice is confirmed facility-wide | Possible; slow; individual share often modest |

The thing driving all of it is that a **Joint Commission–accredited facility** is facing
four independent, documentable problems at once — forged consent, publicly displayed
bedroom cameras, misdirected addiction records with an attorney witness, and religious
discrimination corroborated by their own marketing.

**Their insurer will want this resolved quietly.** That is where leverage comes from.
